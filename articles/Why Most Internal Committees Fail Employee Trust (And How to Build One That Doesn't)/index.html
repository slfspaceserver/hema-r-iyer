Internal Committee & Trust

Why Most Internal Committees Fail Employee Trust (And How to Build One That Doesn't)

Hema R Iyer • June 20, 2026

Creating Safer Workplaces & Protecting Those Who Need It Most

Many companies proudly state that they have an Internal Committee (IC) in place.

Yet, if an employee experiences inappropriate behaviour, would they know whom to contact? Would they trust the process? Would they feel safe enough to report it?

In many workplaces, the answer is no.

That's because having an IC and having an effective IC are two very different things.

An Internal Committee should not be a compliance checkbox tucked away in policy documents. It should be a visible, approachable, and trusted mechanism that employees can rely on when it matters most.

What Is an Internal Committee?

The Internal Committee (IC) is a body constituted under the Prevention of Sexual Harassment (POSH) Act, 2013.

Its primary purpose is to receive and address complaints of sexual harassment in the workplace and ensure that concerns are handled fairly, confidentially, and in accordance with the law.

However, the role of the IC goes beyond investigations.

A well-functioning IC contributes to creating a workplace culture where employees feel safe, respected, and heard.

How Should an IC Be Formed?

Many organizations focus on creating an IC to meet compliance requirements.

But proper formation is equally important.

An Internal Committee should include:

  • A Presiding Officer who is a senior woman employee
  • Employee members who are committed to workplace safety and awareness
  • An External Member with experience in social work, legal knowledge, or issues related to workplace harassment

The purpose of having diverse members is to ensure fairness, credibility, and balanced decision-making.

What Are the Key Responsibilities of an IC?

Many people assume the IC only becomes active when a complaint is received.

In reality, its responsibilities are much broader.

An effective IC should:

  • Receive complaints and acknowledge them appropriately
  • Conduct fair and unbiased inquiries
  • Maintain confidentiality throughout the process
  • Recommend actions based on findings
  • Support awareness and prevention initiatives
  • Encourage employees to understand workplace boundaries and respectful behaviour

A strong IC helps prevent issues, not just respond to them.

Why Employees Hesitate to Approach the IC

Even when organizations have a properly constituted IC, employees may still hesitate to come forward.

Common reasons include:

  • Fear of being judged
  • Concerns about confidentiality
  • Fear of workplace gossip
  • Fear of retaliation
  • Lack of awareness about the process
  • Uncertainty about whether their concern is serious enough

This is why trust is as important as compliance.

Employees must know that concerns will be handled respectfully and professionally.

Common Mistakes Organizations Make

1. Creating an IC Only for Compliance

Some organizations form an IC because the law requires it but do little beyond that.

Employees remain unaware of who the members are or how the committee functions.

2. Lack of Training

An IC is only as effective as its members.

Without proper training, members may struggle with investigations, documentation, confidentiality, and procedural fairness.

3. Poor Visibility

Many employees cannot identify a single IC member in their organization.

If employees don't know whom to approach, the process becomes inaccessible.

4. Treating Every Complaint Casually

Every concern deserves to be treated seriously and professionally.

Even if a matter appears minor initially, it should be handled appropriately.

5. Breach of Confidentiality

Nothing damages trust faster than information being shared beyond those who need to know.

Confidentiality is not just a legal requirement; it is the foundation of employee confidence.

6. Focusing Only on Complaints

The best ICs spend time creating awareness, conducting sessions, and helping employees understand respectful workplace behaviour.

Prevention is always better than intervention.

What Makes an Effective Internal Committee?

An effective IC is not measured by the number of complaints it handles.

It is measured by the confidence employees have in approaching it.

Employees should feel:

  • Safe to speak
  • Confident that they will be heard
  • Assured of confidentiality
  • Trust that the process will be fair

When these elements are present, organizations move beyond compliance and towards creating a genuinely respectful workplace.

Closing Thoughts

An Internal Committee is more than a legal requirement.

It is an important part of an organization's commitment to employee well-being, workplace safety, and respectful behaviour.

The goal is not simply to have an IC.

The goal is to build one that employees trust.

Because a workplace becomes safer not when policies exist, but when people believe those policies will work when they need them.
